## Related Work

**EEG and MEG foundation models.** Self-supervised models pretrained on large, heterogeneous EEG corpora now supply general-purpose representations across montages and tasks. Examples include BIOT (Yang et al., 2023), LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024), EEGPT (Wang et al., 2024), CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025), LUNA (Döner et al., 2025), REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025) and BrainOmni, which also covers MEG (Xiao et al., 2025). Most of these handle variable layouts by attaching learned or coordinate-based positional embeddings to channel tokens, so whatever spatial structure the signal has must be discovered from data. ZUNA (Warner et al., 2026a) and ZUNA1.1 (Warner et al., 2026b) target reconstruction directly. They are diffusion autoencoders with space-time positional encodings that infill or super-resolve arbitrary channel sets, and both report gains over spherical splines. A second line builds biophysics in explicitly, either as a volume-conduction-inspired distance kernel on electrode positions (DeeperBrain; Wang et al., 2026) or as a neural-mass model whose activity is projected through a lead field (NeuroDyn-EEG; Cui et al., 2026). Auditing of EEG foundation models has so far focused on explaining predictions, for instance by mapping attributions into spectral and source space (EEG-PRISM; Shama et al., 2026). We neither build physics in nor explain individual predictions: we ask whether a model trained without explicit physics nonetheless *uses* it, using a black-box behavioural test.

**Channel interpolation and spatial super-resolution.** Spherical splines (Perrin et al., 1989) remain the default for repairing bad channels. Like other kernel smoothers, they predict a missing channel as a fixed linear combination of the observed ones. Deep networks have been trained to upsample sparse montages, both on simulated and evoked-potential data (Kwon et al., 2019) and on a large clinical corpus where CNNs matched or exceeded splines (Svantesson et al., 2021). Physics-based interpolators instead fit equivalent sources with a head model and re-project them to all electrodes, as in RESIT (Dong et al., 2021), or map data between montages by field interpolation (Mellot et al., 2024). These studies show *that* learned and physics-based infill can beat splines. We ask *why*, separating covariance-level smoothing from use of the generative structure of scalp fields.

**Volume conduction, forward models and source localisation.** At EEG frequencies the head acts as a quasi-static volume conductor, so each scalp map is a linear projection of a few compact current sources through the lead field (Hämäläinen et al., 1993; Baillet et al., 2001; Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). Forward fields for multi-shell spherical heads can be computed quickly and accurately (Berg & Scherg, 1994), and standard toolboxes such as MNE implement spherical alongside realistic head models (Gramfort et al., 2014). Subspace scanning methods, notably recursive and RAP-MUSIC (Mosher & Leahy, 1998, 1999), localise a small number of dipoles by scanning lead-field topographies against the signal subspace. Rather than localising sources, we use these tools to generate "possible" scalp patterns and to build an oracle infiller with access to the true lead field, which shows how much physics-aware infill can gain.

**Probing foundation models for physics and world models.** Vafa et al. (2025) test whether a foundation model has internalised a postulated world model by adapting it to synthetic tasks and checking whether its inductive bias aligns with that model. Violation-of-expectation designs, borrowed from developmental psychology, read greater surprise on physically impossible events as evidence of physical knowledge. They have been applied to models trained on simulated scenes (Piloto et al., 2022) and to self-supervised video models (Garrido et al., 2025). In weather prediction, Hakim & Masanam (2024) probe a deep-learning forecast model with idealised dynamical experiments to check that its responses are physically consistent. We bring this approach to EEG, and we match our possible and impossible stimuli on second-order statistics so that differences in performance cannot be explained by covariance-based smoothing.

**Surrogate-data hypothesis testing.** The method of surrogate data tests a null hypothesis by comparing a statistic on observed data with its distribution over surrogates that preserve exactly the properties the null specifies, such as the power spectrum of a linear Gaussian process (Theiler et al., 1992). Multivariate surrogates also preserve the cross-correlations among simultaneously recorded channels (Prichard & Theiler, 1994), and Schreiber & Schmitz (2000) review constrained-realisation variants. Our "impossible" patterns are spatial Gaussian surrogates drawn from N(0, Σ) with the same ensemble covariance as the dipole fields. Every fixed linear infiller, splines included, therefore has the same expected error on both sets, so any gap must come from structure beyond second order. We carry the surrogate logic over from time series to scalp topographies and use it to audit a learned model rather than a dynamical system.

**Input normalisation.** EEG pipelines differ in whether amplitudes are standardised within each channel or jointly across channels, and the choice of normalisation alone can rival dedicated domain-adaptation methods (Apicella et al., 2023). Truong & Delorme (2025) point out that cross-channel normalisation preserves the relative voltage scaling between channels, whereas within-channel normalisation preserves only each channel's temporal structure. They also find that the best scheme depends on whether training is supervised or self-supervised. Neither study considers channel infill. For infill, we show that per-channel statistics computed with the masked channel included leak its amplitude, and we test whether per-channel normalisation, which discards the amplitude topography that volume conduction constrains, keeps ZUNA from exploiting the physics.
